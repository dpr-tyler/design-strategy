<SectionLabel>Why it matters now</SectionLabel>

The growth team model that Ellis pioneered at Dropbox and evangelized across Silicon Valley has become standard practice — but most implementations miss the core principle that made it work: the combination of analytical rigor, cross-functional authority, and high experiment velocity. As product-led growth has become the default GTM motion for SaaS companies, and as AI tools have made it possible to instrument and analyze user behavior at unprecedented depth, the growth discipline is more relevant than ever. Ellis and Brown's framework is both a philosophy (growth is a process, not a campaign) and a practice (how to structure the team, run the meetings, and prioritize experiments).

<SectionLabel>Core frameworks</SectionLabel>

## The Growth Team Structure

Growth teams are cross-functional squads that own growth metrics across the full funnel, operating outside the normal product roadmap process. A canonical growth team includes a growth lead (who owns the metrics and sets direction), a product manager, engineers, designers, data analysts, and marketers — all focused on a specific growth metric (often activation, retention, or referral). What makes growth teams different from product teams is their relationship to experimentation: where product teams build features, growth teams run experiments. The cadence is different (weekly experiment cycles vs. quarterly roadmap cycles), the success criteria are different (statistically significant metric lifts vs. shipped features), and the operating model is different (hypothesis-driven vs. customer-request-driven).

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Growth is a process, not a campaign. A growth team that runs 52 experiments per year — one per week — will outperform a team that runs 12 well-crafted campaigns, because the cumulative learning from experiments compounds in ways that campaigns cannot.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The Aha Moment and Activation

The single highest-leverage area for most growth teams is activation — getting new users to their "aha moment" as quickly and reliably as possible. The aha moment is the specific experience in which a new user first receives the core value the product promises. Ellis identified Dropbox's aha moment as the first time a user actually synced a file across two devices; Facebook's was adding 7 friends in 10 days. The aha moment is a product discovery, not an assumption — finding it requires analyzing cohort data to identify the behaviors that most strongly correlate with long-term retention, then designing the onboarding experience to drive users to that behavior as rapidly as possible.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Every product has a specific user action or experience that is most predictive of long-term retention. Finding and optimizing for that moment — the aha moment — is the highest-leverage activation intervention available.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The ICE Scoring Framework

With many possible experiments and limited resources, prioritization is the most consequential daily decision a growth team makes. ICE scoring provides a simple, shared framework: rate each experiment idea on **Impact** (how much will this move the target metric if it works?), **Confidence** (how confident are you that it will work?), and **Ease** (how easy is it to run the experiment?). Each dimension is scored 1–10, and the ICE score is the average. ICE is intentionally imprecise — its value is not producing a correct rank order but forcing explicit reasoning about each dimension, surfacing disagreements, and preventing the team from defaulting to the loudest opinion or the most recent data.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Experiment prioritization should be explicit and shared, not implicit and individual. Any forcing function that makes the team articulate why an experiment is worth running — before running it — produces better experiment portfolios than intuition-driven queuing.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Retention as the Foundation

Ellis and Brown argue that retention is the foundation on which acquisition and revenue are built — and that teams that invest in acquisition before achieving healthy retention are burning money to fill a leaky bucket. Retention analysis begins with cohort analysis: do users who joined in month N still use the product in month N+6? If retention curves flatten at a healthy level, the product has retention; if they continue declining toward zero, the product has a retention problem that no amount of acquisition will fix. The retention fix is almost always a product fix — improving the product's ability to deliver ongoing value — not a communications or re-engagement fix.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** No growth strategy survives a broken retention curve. If the cohort analysis shows users churning to zero, the growth team's first job is a product intervention — not a re-engagement campaign.
</FrameworkCallout>

<SectionLabel>Mental models</SectionLabel>

**The growth lever hierarchy.** Not all growth levers are equal. Retention improvements compound — better retention means every new user acquired stays longer, increasing LTV and word-of-mouth. Activation improvements multiply — they determine how many acquired users become retained users. Acquisition improvements add. Revenue improvements extract. The order of priority for most early-stage products should be: retention, then activation, then acquisition, then revenue — not the reverse.

**Language-market fit.** Before running acquisition experiments, Ellis argues for finding language-market fit: the specific words and phrases that most resonate with the target customer when describing the product's value. A/B testing landing page headlines, onboarding copy, and email subject lines across hundreds of small experiments can identify the language that dramatically improves conversion — and that language reveals how customers actually think about the problem the product solves.

**Viral coefficient and cycle time.** Viral growth depends on two variables: the viral coefficient (how many new users does each existing user generate?) and the cycle time (how long does the viral loop take to complete?). A viral coefficient above 1 means the product grows without additional acquisition investment. But even a coefficient below 1 provides meaningful compounding if the cycle time is short. Optimizing viral loops requires both increasing the coefficient (better share mechanics, better incentives) and reducing cycle time (faster sharing, faster registration, faster activation).

**The bright spots method.** In any user base, some segment of users is experiencing dramatically better outcomes than average. Identifying these bright spots — users with unusually high engagement, retention, or referral rates — and reverse-engineering why they are succeeding reveals the path to improving outcomes for the broader population. The growth team's job is to design experiences that move more users toward the bright spot behaviors.

<SectionLabel>The one thing</SectionLabel>

Build a cross-functional team that runs a weekly growth experiment cycle, prioritizes retention above acquisition, and maintains an unbroken commitment to letting data — not opinion — determine what gets built next.
