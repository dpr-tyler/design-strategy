<SectionLabel>Why it matters now</SectionLabel>

Product strategy is prediction. Every roadmap decision is a bet on which problems matter, which solutions will work, and which markets will emerge. Most organizations treat these bets as expressions of vision or judgment — opaque, unchallengeable, and unmeasurable. Tetlock's research shows that prediction quality varies dramatically across individuals, that the best predictors use identifiable and learnable methods, and that accuracy can be tracked, scored, and improved over time. For product leaders who want to improve their decision quality rather than just their confidence, this book is a practical manual.

<SectionLabel>Core frameworks</SectionLabel>

## The Superforecaster Profile

Tetlock's decade-long Good Judgment Project identified a small group of "superforecasters" who dramatically outperformed professional intelligence analysts — even analysts with access to classified information. What distinguished them was not domain expertise but cognitive style: they were actively open-minded, comfortable with uncertainty, genuinely curious about being wrong, and disciplined about expressing beliefs as probabilities rather than assertions. They did not say "the market will shift" — they said "I believe there is a 70% chance the market shifts in the next 18 months, and here is what would update that estimate."

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Expressing beliefs as probabilities is not hedging — it is precision. A probability invites calibration and updating; an assertion invites defense.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Foxes vs. Hedgehogs

Borrowing Isaiah Berlin's metaphor, Tetlock distinguishes hedgehogs (who know one big thing and explain everything through it) from foxes (who know many small things and combine them pragmatically). The most confident, prominent pundits — those who appear on television and write bestselling books about how the future will unfold — are disproportionately hedgehogs. And hedgehogs are, on average, worse forecasters than foxes. Domain expertise tends to make people more like hedgehogs — more committed to their explanatory framework, more likely to assimilate contradictory evidence rather than update on it.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** The confident expert with a grand unified theory of your market is not more likely to be right than the thoughtful generalist — they are probably less likely, because their confidence is inversely correlated with their calibration.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Calibration and the Brier Score

Superforecasters are calibrated — when they say something has a 70% chance of happening, it happens about 70% of the time. Most people are miscalibrated in predictable ways: overconfident on hard questions, underconfident on easy ones, and systematically prone to placing too many predictions in the "roughly 50/50" bucket to avoid being clearly wrong. Tetlock measures forecast quality with the Brier score, which rewards both accuracy and confidence: a correct prediction made at 55% probability is worth less than one made at 85% probability. This scoring creates real incentives to think carefully rather than hedge.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Calibration is a measurable property of a track record, not a feeling. Saying "I'm usually right" is not evidence of calibration — keeping and scoring a prediction log is.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The Dragonfly Eye and Active Updating

Superforecasters use what Tetlock calls a "dragonfly eye" — they aggregate multiple, diverse perspectives rather than committing to a single view. They identify base rates (how often do things like this happen?), then adjust for specific features of the case at hand. They distinguish inside-view factors (what is unique about this situation) from outside-view factors (what historically happens in situations like this). Crucially, they update actively: when new information arrives, they revise their probability estimates rather than rationalizing the new data into their existing view.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** The most important forecasting discipline is not forming a good initial view — it is updating that view quickly and proportionally when evidence changes.
</FrameworkCallout>

<SectionLabel>Mental models</SectionLabel>

**Fermi estimation as a forecasting method.** Superforecasters break hard questions into smaller, more tractable sub-questions that can be estimated from first principles. How large is the addressable market? Break it into: how many companies are in this category, what fraction face this problem, what fraction would pay to solve it, what would they pay? Each sub-estimate is uncertain, but combined estimates are often more accurate than a single top-down guess because errors partially cancel.

**Premortem as calibration tool.** Before committing to a decision, imagine it is two years later and the outcome was a failure. Work backward: what went wrong? The premortem forces consideration of the inside-view scenarios that motivated the decision — but specifically surfaces the failure modes that motivated reasoning tends to skip. It is a structured way of finding the scenarios you are implicitly assigning near-zero probability that might deserve 10-20%.

**Reference class forecasting.** Before estimating how long your project will take or how well your launch will perform, find comparable past cases and look at their distribution of outcomes. The base rate is your prior. Your specific case can adjust from there — but adjustments should be modest unless you have genuinely strong evidence that your case is structurally different. Most inside views are not structurally different; they just feel different because you can see them in detail.

**Scope insensitivity.** People respond similarly to problems of vastly different scale. The emotional response to "1,000 users affected" is not meaningfully different from "100,000 users affected" unless you force a numerical comparison. Good forecasters resist this by working in numbers throughout their analysis — not because numbers are more emotionally compelling but because they force genuine discrimination between cases of different magnitude.
