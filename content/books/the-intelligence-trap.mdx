<SectionLabel>Why it matters now</SectionLabel>

The highest-stakes decisions in product organizations are typically made by the most experienced, high-IQ people in the room. This feels reassuring. Robson's synthesis of research across psychology, medicine, and business reveals it should be cause for specific concern: the same capabilities that make someone intellectually powerful — pattern recognition, rapid synthesis, confident argumentation — also make them more effective at defending wrong conclusions, less likely to notice when they have left their domain of genuine competence, and more likely to discount evidence that contradicts their existing model. Smart, expert teams do not make fewer mistakes; they make fewer obvious mistakes and more sophisticated, harder-to-detect ones.

<SectionLabel>Core frameworks</SectionLabel>

## Dysrationalia: The Intelligence-Rationality Gap

Robson draws on the work of Keith Stanovich to distinguish intelligence (the ability to learn and solve problems) from rationality (the ability to hold calibrated beliefs and make good decisions). These are distinct and only weakly correlated. High-IQ individuals are no better, and often worse, at recognizing cognitive biases in their own reasoning, because they have more cognitive resources to deploy in constructing arguments for conclusions they have already reached. Stanovich calls this "dysrationalia" — the condition of being intelligent but systematically irrational. The most dangerous form is motivated reasoning executed at high speed and sophistication.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Intelligence amplifies whatever thinking processes are in place. Paired with good epistemic habits, it produces excellent reasoning. Paired with motivated reasoning, it produces more sophisticated rationalizations.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Earned Dogmatism

Robson identifies a pattern he calls "earned dogmatism" — the tendency for experts to feel licensed, by virtue of their expertise, to hold strong views with less evidence than a non-expert would require. The logic feels sound: I have spent decades in this field, my intuitions have been validated repeatedly, I do not need to justify every judgment from first principles. But this reasoning fails to account for domain boundaries: expertise is calibrated within a specific context, and the expert's confidence does not degrade gracefully when they step outside it. The most dangerous expert is the one who cannot distinguish the edge of their competence.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Confidence should track calibration, not experience. An expert with 20 years in a domain should be very confident within their validated range and explicitly uncertain outside it — but expertise tends to produce generalized confidence instead.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The Bias Blind Spot

Research consistently shows that people believe they are less biased than others — and that belief is strongest among the most intelligent. The mechanism is clear: recognizing bias in others requires pattern recognition (which smart people do well); recognizing bias in yourself requires stepping outside your own reasoning process (which is harder the more fluent that process is). The practical implication is that bias training and awareness programs that focus on recognizing bias in general have limited impact on self-assessments and actual decisions.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Knowing about biases does not reduce them — and for intelligent people, it can increase confidence that they are the exception. Structural interventions (checklists, pre-mortems, outside reviewers, diverse teams) work better than awareness.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Evidence-Based Wisdom

Robson's prescriptive framework draws on research into what separates good from great decision-makers. The key practices are: intellectual humility (genuinely holding views as probabilistic hypotheses rather than identities), actively open-minded thinking (seeking out disconfirming evidence rather than just confirming evidence), reflective thinking (slowing down deliberation before high-stakes judgments), and what he calls "evidence-based wisdom" — the combination of broad knowledge with careful epistemic practice. These practices do not emerge automatically from expertise or intelligence; they must be explicitly cultivated.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Intellectual humility is not an absence of confidence — it is the precise calibration of confidence to evidence. The goal is not to doubt everything but to hold each belief with exactly as much certainty as the evidence warrants.
</FrameworkCallout>

<SectionLabel>Mental models</SectionLabel>

**Motivated skepticism vs. motivated credulity.** When we encounter evidence we like, we ask "can I believe this?" When we encounter evidence that contradicts our view, we ask "must I believe this?" Both are strategies for reaching preferred conclusions while maintaining the appearance of rationality. Recognizing which mode you are in when evaluating a piece of evidence is the first step to correcting for it — and it requires deliberate, effortful attention rather than the automatic processing that intelligence tends to accelerate.

**The sycophancy trap.** Expert leaders in organizations often receive information that has been pre-filtered, softened, and shaped to match what subordinates believe the leader wants to hear. Over time, the leader's model of reality drifts away from actual reality, and because the leader is intelligent, the model remains internally coherent even as it becomes externally wrong. The structural fix is explicit investment in disconfirming information channels: anonymous feedback, red teams, structured devil's advocacy, external advisors without political skin in the game.

**Solution aversion.** People sometimes resist accepting the existence of a problem because they dislike the available solutions. Climate skepticism is partly driven by aversion to the policy solutions climate science implies. In product organizations, teams sometimes dismiss evidence of a fundamental product problem because the implied fix — a major rearchitecture, an organizational restructure, a pricing model change — is too costly to contemplate. The evidence is not evaluated on its merits; it is evaluated on whether accepting it requires an unwanted action.

**Transfer of expertise.** Skills and intuitions developed in one domain do not transfer automatically to structurally different domains. A brilliant technical architect making product strategy decisions, a highly successful consumer product leader entering enterprise, an expert in mature markets managing an early-stage product — each is operating partly on calibrated expertise and partly on intuition borrowed from a different context. The confidence does not degrade; the calibration does. Naming this explicitly in team discussions reduces its impact.

</PullQuote>

"Smart people are more adept at rationalizing whatever they already believe. They're better at constructing arguments, and they use that ability to defend their existing views rather than to examine them."

</PullQuote>

<SectionLabel>The one thing</SectionLabel>

The smarter the team, the more important it is to build structural checks against motivated reasoning — because intelligence makes rationalization faster and more convincing, not less likely.
