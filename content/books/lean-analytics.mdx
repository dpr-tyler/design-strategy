<SectionLabel>Why it matters now</SectionLabel>

In a world where product teams can instrument everything, the proliferation of dashboards has not made decision-making clearer — it has made it murkier. Croll and Yoskovitz wrote the book before "data-driven" became a cliché, and their core argument is still contrarian: most metrics are vanity metrics that make you feel good without telling you if the business is working. The One Metric That Matters (OMTM) framework is a forcing function for intellectual honesty — it demands that teams identify the number that most precisely captures whether they are making real progress at this specific stage of the business. For product teams buried in analytics tools and A/B testing frameworks, this discipline is as urgently needed as ever.

<SectionLabel>Core frameworks</SectionLabel>

## The One Metric That Matters (OMTM)

The OMTM is not a permanent metric — it is the single most important number for the current stage of the business, right now. It changes as the stage changes. The discipline is twofold: first, forcing alignment around one number rather than a balanced scorecard of metrics that let teams rationalize any outcome; second, identifying the right metric for the current stage rather than the metric that is easiest to improve. A good OMTM is a leading indicator of the business outcome you care about, is something you can influence with product decisions, and is honest — it cannot be gamed without actually improving the thing it measures. Teams that operate with a clear OMTM move faster because every experiment has an unambiguous success criterion.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Having one metric that matters is not a limitation — it is a forcing function. A team that cannot agree on one metric has not agreed on what success looks like, which means experiments, roadmaps, and resources cannot be prioritized with clarity.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The Lean Analytics Stages

Croll and Yoskovitz map startup progress to five stages, each with a different OMTM focus. **Empathy** (do you understand the problem?): the metric is qualitative — depth of validated customer pain. **Stickiness** (does your product create habit?): retention and engagement metrics dominate; growth is irrelevant if people don't come back. **Virality** (does your product spread?): viral coefficient and cycle time. **Revenue** (can you monetize?): revenue per customer, conversion rate, and margin. **Scale** (can you grow without breaking?): cost of customer acquisition relative to lifetime value, and operational efficiency. The critical insight is that optimizing for a later-stage metric too early — chasing revenue before you have stickiness, chasing virality before you have a product worth spreading — creates a leaky bucket with a marketing hose pointed at it.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Premature scaling is the number one cause of startup failure. The right metric for your stage is not the metric that impresses investors — it is the metric that tells you whether the foundation is solid enough to build on.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Vanity vs. Actionable Metrics

Vanity metrics make you look good without telling you whether the business is working. Page views, registered users, downloads, and press mentions are classic vanity metrics — they can go up while the business deteriorates. Actionable metrics are tied to behaviors you can change with decisions, measure something that is actually correlated with business health, and produce clear next actions. The test: can two people look at this metric and agree on what to do differently? If not, it is probably a vanity metric. Croll and Yoskovitz also distinguish **correlated** metrics (two things that move together) from **causal** metrics (changing one actually changes the other). Experiments that confuse correlation with causation optimize for the wrong thing.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Vanity metrics are not harmless — they are actively dangerous because they create confidence that delays the discovery of a failing business model. The discipline of choosing actionable metrics is the discipline of choosing honesty over comfort.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Business Model Analytics Archetypes

Not all businesses have the same critical metrics. Croll and Yoskovitz identify six business model archetypes, each with distinct metric priorities: **E-commerce** (conversion rate, average order value, repeat purchase rate), **SaaS/subscription** (churn, MRR, expansion revenue, LTV/CAC), **Free mobile app** (DAU/MAU ratio, session length, ARPU), **Media** (pageviews, ad revenue per visitor, content engagement), **User-generated content** (content creation rate, quality of content, virality), and **Two-sided marketplace** (liquidity, GMV, take rate). Understanding your archetype is the prerequisite to identifying the right OMTM — and most product teams borrow metrics from the wrong archetype.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** The right metrics depend entirely on the business model. Applying SaaS metrics to a marketplace, or media metrics to a SaaS product, produces misleading conclusions that drive bad decisions.
</FrameworkCallout>

<SectionLabel>Mental models</SectionLabel>

**The pirate metrics (AARRR) as a diagnostic.** Dave McClure's AARRR framework — Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue — maps the customer journey to five conversion stages. Its value in the context of Lean Analytics is as a diagnostic: by measuring each stage, you identify which conversion is leaking most badly and focus improvement there. Teams that optimize acquisition when activation is broken are filling a tub with the drain open.

**The line vs. the number.** A single metric at a point in time is a number. A metric over time is a line, and the line reveals whether the business is improving, deteriorating, or plateauing. The slope of the line matters more than the absolute value — a low absolute number that is trending sharply upward is a healthier signal than a high absolute number that is flat or declining.

**Segment before you average.** Averages hide the most important signals. An average session length of 4 minutes across all users might hide a segment with 12-minute sessions (highly engaged, worth understanding) and a segment with 30-second sessions (who are they, and why are they churning?). The OMTM framework applies at the segment level, not just the aggregate — and the segment-level analysis often reveals the growth wedge.

**Good vs. bad engagement.** Not all engagement is created equal. Engagement on your core value loop is a health signal; engagement with support articles, error messages, or retry flows is a failure signal masquerading as engagement. Measuring "time in product" without distinguishing productive engagement from frustrated engagement produces dangerously misleading conclusions.

<SectionLabel>The one thing</SectionLabel>

Identify the single metric that most honestly captures whether your product is working at this exact stage of your business — then refuse to celebrate any other number until that one is moving in the right direction.
