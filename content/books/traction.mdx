<SectionLabel>Why it matters now</SectionLabel>

The build-it-and-they-will-come fallacy remains one of the most persistent and expensive mistakes in product development. Weinberg and Mares wrote Traction as the canonical counter-argument: half of your time should be spent on product, and half on traction. For AI-native startups in particular, where building the core product has become easier and faster than ever, traction — reliable, repeatable customer acquisition — is the actual scarcity. The Bullseye Framework and the 19-channel taxonomy give product and growth teams a systematic method for finding their growth engine, rather than copying the tactics of a successful company in a different context with different unit economics.

<SectionLabel>Core frameworks</SectionLabel>

## The 19 Traction Channels

Weinberg and Mares identify 19 distinct channels through which companies acquire customers: **Viral Marketing**, **Public Relations**, **Unconventional PR** (stunts and events), **Search Engine Marketing (SEM)**, **Social and Display Ads**, **Offline Ads**, **Search Engine Optimization (SEO)**, **Content Marketing**, **Email Marketing**, **Engineering as Marketing** (tools and calculators), **Targeting Blogs**, **Business Development**, **Sales**, **Affiliate Programs**, **Existing Platforms** (app stores, marketplaces), **Trade Shows**, **Offline Events**, **Speaking Engagements**, and **Community Building**. The critical insight is not the list itself — it is that at any stage of a company, almost every channel will feel obvious or wrong, and the channel that actually works is rarely the one that felt most obvious. Systematic testing is required.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Most teams pursue the traction channels that feel natural to the founders (often content, social, or viral) and ignore the channels that might actually work for their specific product, market, and unit economics. The channel that works is an empirical question, not an intuitive one.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The Bullseye Framework

The Bullseye Framework is Weinberg's systematic method for finding your best traction channel. It proceeds in three rings. **Outer ring — brainstorm:** Consider every channel, generate testable ideas for each, and identify which channels seem most promising based on your product, market, and resources. **Middle ring — test:** Run cheap, fast experiments in the top 3–5 channels simultaneously. The goal is not to run these channels at scale — it is to generate a signal about which channel has the best potential. Each test should cost no more than a few thousand dollars and a few weeks of time. **Inner ring — focus:** Once a channel shows meaningful signal, put the majority of your resources into scaling that channel while you maintain minimum viable testing in others. Focus is the engine of traction; premature diversification is its enemy.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Traction channels should be tested systematically and simultaneously, not sequentially. Testing one at a time takes too long and does not generate the comparative signal needed to make a good prioritization decision.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The 50% Rule

Weinberg's most contrarian prescription is the 50% rule: founders and product teams should spend 50% of their time building the product and 50% finding traction. This is not a metaphor — it is a structural commitment to treating customer acquisition as coequal with product development. The intuition against this rule is strong: the product is not ready, traction experiments will be wasted on a bad product, and the team needs focus. Weinberg's response is that discovering your traction channel early produces information that shapes the product, not just the go-to-market. Companies that delay traction work until the product is "ready" find themselves with a polished product and no distribution.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Distribution is a product problem, not a sales problem. The traction channel you can scale shapes which features matter, which customer segments to prioritize, and what "done" looks like for the product.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Traction Channel Saturation

Every traction channel eventually saturates — the cost of acquisition in any given channel increases as competition increases, audiences fatigue, and the marginal customer becomes harder to reach. The best companies are always running experiments in secondary channels while scaling their primary channel, so they have a next channel ready before the current one degrades. This is the traction equivalent of the product portfolio: you need exploratory bets running even when the current growth engine is working well, because by the time the current engine slows, it is too late to start the exploratory work.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Traction channel diversification is not the goal — focus is. But the exploratory work that finds the next channel must start well before the current channel saturates.
</FrameworkCallout>

<SectionLabel>Mental models</SectionLabel>

**Traction as validation, not just acquisition.** Early traction work is not just about getting customers — it is about validating that a scalable path to customers exists. A product with 1,000 users acquired through a channel that cannot scale to 100,000 does not have traction; it has early adopters. The scalability of the acquisition channel is part of the business model, not separate from it.

**Qualitative traction signal.** Not all traction signal is quantitative. Talking to customers who came through a particular channel reveals whether those customers are the right customers — high-fit, high-retention, high-referral customers are worth more than high-volume, low-quality customers from a less targeted channel. The best traction experiments measure quality of acquisition, not just quantity.

**Adjacent channel thinking.** Channels that appear distinct often share underlying mechanics. Content marketing and SEO are deeply intertwined. Speaking engagements and PR amplify each other. Business development and existing platforms often overlap. Mapping the interactions between channels — and designing experiments that test several adjacent channels simultaneously — produces more signal per dollar than treating each channel as completely independent.

**The traction-product feedback loop.** What you learn in traction experiments should feed back into product decisions. If the channel that works is SEO, the product roadmap should include features that generate organic search content (calculators, templates, public-facing outputs). If the channel that works is bottom-up SaaS virality, the product roadmap should prioritize in-product sharing and collaboration features. Traction strategy and product strategy are not separate.

<SectionLabel>The one thing</SectionLabel>

Run cheap, fast experiments across multiple traction channels simultaneously — and when one shows disproportionate signal, concentrate resources there before the window closes.
