<SectionLabel>Why it matters now</SectionLabel>

The build trap is not a startup problem — it is an organizational problem that becomes more acute as companies scale. As engineering teams grow and delivery capacity increases, the pressure to fill that capacity with features intensifies. Sprint velocity becomes a surrogate for progress; roadmaps become feature backlogs; product management becomes ticket writing. Perri's book is the most direct diagnosis of this failure mode and the clearest prescription for escaping it. For product leaders in scaling companies navigating the gap between "we ship a lot" and "we're not growing," this book provides both the vocabulary for the problem and a structural model for the fix.

<SectionLabel>Core frameworks</SectionLabel>

## The Build Trap Defined

The build trap occurs when an organization measures success by the quantity of features shipped rather than by the outcomes those features produce. In the build trap, product managers are essentially project managers — translating business requests into specifications and tracking delivery. Roadmaps are lists of features with dates. Success is shipping on schedule. The customer's actual behavior — whether the feature was used, whether it solved the problem, whether it changed the metric it was supposed to change — is nobody's explicit job to measure. Perri identifies three types of bad product managers that the build trap produces: the **Mini-CEO** who makes unilateral decisions without customer evidence; the **Waiter** who takes orders from stakeholders without pushing back; and the **Former Founder** who builds based on personal conviction rather than market signal.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** The build trap is not caused by bad intentions — it is caused by organizational systems that reward output (features shipped) rather than outcomes (customer behavior changed). Escaping it requires changing the system, not just the mindset.
</FrameworkCallout>

## The Product Kata

Perri adapts Toyota's Improvement Kata to product development as the Product Kata: a structured four-step cycle for moving from current state to desired outcome. **Step 1 — Understand the direction:** What is the strategic intent? What outcome are we trying to achieve, and why does it matter? **Step 2 — Analyze the current condition:** Where are we now, and what is the gap between current state and desired outcome? **Step 3 — Define the next target condition:** What is the next measurable milestone on the way to the outcome? **Step 4 — Experiment toward the target:** What is the next experiment that will move us from current state to target condition? This cycle runs at the product team level — not the executive level — and it produces a continuous stream of experiments rather than a static roadmap.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** The Product Kata replaces the roadmap as the unit of planning. Instead of committing to features in advance, teams commit to outcomes and design experiments that test the path to those outcomes.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Outcomes vs. Outputs

The central distinction in the book is between outcomes (changes in customer or business behavior that indicate value was delivered) and outputs (things the team produced — features, code, documentation). Outputs are necessary but not sufficient; they only have value if they produce outcomes. A feature that ships but is never used, does not change customer behavior, and does not move a metric is pure output with zero outcome. The transition from output thinking to outcome thinking requires product teams to define, before building, what customer behavior change would indicate the feature succeeded — and to measure whether that behavior change occurs after launch.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Define the behavioral outcome you expect before you build. After launch, measure whether that behavior changed. If it did not, the feature failed regardless of whether it shipped on time.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Product Strategy as a System

Perri argues that product strategy is not a roadmap — it is a system of choices that connects company vision to team experiments. A good strategy system has four layers: **Company vision** (the long-term aspirational state), **Strategic intent** (the current 1–3 year focus that moves toward the vision), **Product initiatives** (the problems to solve this quarter that address the strategic intent), and **Options** (the possible solutions the team will test against each initiative). Most organizations have vision and roadmaps but are missing the middle layers — strategic intent and problem framing — which means teams have no principled way to evaluate whether a proposed feature is worth building.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Without a clear hierarchy from vision to strategic intent to initiative to option, product teams have no principled way to say no — and a team that cannot say no to features is in the build trap.
</FrameworkCallout>

<SectionLabel>Mental models</SectionLabel>

**The product manager as value maximizer.** Perri defines the product manager's job as maximizing value for customers and the business — not as managing the delivery process. This reframing changes everything: the PM's job is to identify which problems are worth solving, find solutions that address those problems, and measure whether the solutions worked. Delivery coordination is a byproduct of this work, not its purpose.

**The opportunity solution tree.** Before building, product teams should map the opportunity space: given the desired outcome, what are the possible opportunities (customer problems) that would produce it? For each opportunity, what are the possible solutions? For each solution, what are the possible experiments to test it? This tree makes the reasoning from outcome to experiment explicit and prevents teams from jumping to solutions before understanding the opportunity space.

**Organizational structure as product strategy.** The way a product organization is structured determines what it can produce. Teams organized around features build features. Teams organized around customer journeys build journeys. Teams organized around outcomes run experiments. Perri argues that the team structure should reflect the strategic intent — and that most organizations are structured in ways that guarantee output-orientation, regardless of what the mission statement says.

**The strategy deployment gap.** In most organizations, there is a significant gap between the strategy articulated at the executive level and the decisions made at the team level. Executives set strategic intent but cannot translate it into product initiatives; teams receive feature requests but lack the strategic context to evaluate them. The product strategy system exists to close this gap — not through cascading plans but through shared vocabulary, shared metrics, and structured decision rights at each layer.
