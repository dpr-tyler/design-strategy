<SectionLabel>Why it matters now</SectionLabel>

Marquet's account of transforming the USS Santa Fe from the worst-performing submarine in the U.S. Navy to the best reads as a precise critique of how most product organizations are still structured: a small group at the top makes decisions, and a large group below implements them. As organizations grow and the complexity of software products outpaces the cognitive bandwidth of any leadership team, the leader-leader model Marquet describes — where decision-making authority lives with the people closest to the information — becomes not just compelling but structurally necessary. The alternative is a bottleneck that scales with headcount and slows everything down.

---

<SectionLabel>Core frameworks</SectionLabel>

## Leader-Leader vs. Leader-Follower

The conventional model of organizational leadership is leader-follower: a small number of people with authority make decisions, and a larger number of people execute them. Marquet argues this model is not just inefficient — it is structurally fragile, because it concentrates organizational intelligence in the same people who are furthest from operational reality. The leader-leader alternative pushes decision-making authority to the people with the most relevant information, regardless of rank. On the Santa Fe, this meant enlisted crew members who previously asked permission before taking any action were instead announcing their intent and proceeding unless stopped. The shift from "request permission to" to "I intend to" is not semantic — it moves the locus of thinking from the top of the organization to the point of action.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** "I intend to" is the linguistic marker of a leader-leader organization. When people announce decisions rather than requesting permission, they are exercising judgment — and the organization is growing more capable, not just more compliant.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Control, Competence, and Clarity

Marquet's framework for making leader-leader work in practice rests on three pillars. Control — the ability to make decisions — can only be pushed downward sustainably if competence and clarity accompany it. Competence means people have the technical knowledge to make good decisions in their domain. Clarity means they understand the organization's goals and values well enough to make decisions that align with leadership intent even when specific guidance is absent. The failure mode most organizations encounter when trying to delegate is skipping one of these pillars: pushing control down without building competence produces bad decisions; pushing control down without clarity produces locally optimized decisions that diverge from organizational direction.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Delegation without competence and clarity is abdication. Control can only be safely pushed down when the people receiving it have both the technical capability to exercise it well and a thorough understanding of what the organization is trying to achieve.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Short, Early Conversations

One of the operational mechanisms Marquet introduces is a deliberate practice of short, early conversations — specifically, the "I intend to" briefing before any significant action. Rather than long after-action reviews that analyze what went wrong, the Santa Fe developed a culture of announcing planned actions far enough in advance that leaders could course-correct before the cost of correction became high. This is a direct application of the principle that short feedback cycles compound: catching a misalignment at the "I intend to" stage costs one conversation; catching it after the action has been taken can cost weeks. For product organizations, this translates to the practice of socializing plans in their early, malleable state rather than presenting finished work for approval.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** The cost of a conversation scales with proximity to execution. Catching problems in the planning stage costs a conversation; catching them after shipping costs a sprint. Leader-leader cultures favor early, short feedback over late, expensive correction.
</FrameworkCallout>

## Resist the Urge to Give Solutions

Marquet's most counterintuitive prescription for leaders making the transition to leader-leader is to actively resist the temptation to provide answers. When a subordinate brings a problem, the leader's instinct is to solve it — and this instinct, repeated at scale, trains the organization to stop thinking and start waiting. Marquet instead coaches leaders to respond to problems with questions: "What do you think we should do?" "What options have you considered?" "What would you recommend?" Over time, this pattern shifts the cognitive work from the top of the organization to the edges, which is where the relevant information lives. The leader's role becomes one of framing, questioning, and creating conditions for good decisions rather than making all the decisions themselves.

<FrameworkCallout>
**Key principle:** Every time a leader gives a solution instead of asking a question, they are training the organization to defer rather than think. The most important habit change for leader-leader transformation is learning to ask before answering.
</FrameworkCallout>

<SectionLabel>Mental models</SectionLabel>

**Psychological ownership precedes accountability.** Organizations that demand accountability without first creating genuine ownership produce a culture of blame rather than responsibility. Marquet's crew became accountable because they were first given actual control over outcomes — accountability followed naturally from ownership rather than being imposed from above.

**Mechanisms over motivation.** Marquet explicitly argues against using emotional appeals to drive performance. Motivation through speeches and exhortations is unreliable and doesn't compound. Structural mechanisms — changing the language people use, altering the sequence of briefings, creating specific forums for announcing intent — are durable because they embed the new behavior in process rather than relying on discretionary effort.

**Technical competence as a leadership prerequisite.** A consistent theme is that leaders who don't understand the technical substance of their team's work cannot effectively delegate. Marquet spent significant time learning the Santa Fe's systems specifically because he could not push control down without being able to evaluate whether the judgments being made were sound. For product leaders, this argues strongly for maintaining technical and domain literacy rather than retreating entirely into process management.

The fastest way to scale organizational intelligence is to push decision-making authority to the people with the most relevant information — and then build the competence and clarity that makes that safe to do.
